Date and Time Handling

An exam timetable, a library due date, a countdown to results, a clock in the corner of the screen: dates and times are on almost every useful page. JavaScript stores each moment as one large number and gives you the Date object to make, read, change, compare and display it. This chapter teaches all five, and the few traps — a month counted from zero, a date string that quietly means midnight at Greenwich — that catch nearly everyone once.

62.1 One moment, one number

Your lecture slide says it in one line: JavaScript's Date object represents a specific point in time. Not a day, not a page of a calendar — one exact moment, such as "the instant the CSE326 exam begins".

How does a computer hold a moment? The way it holds everything: as a number. JavaScript picked one fixed starting moment, midnight at the start of 1 January 1970 in UTC, and called it the epoch. Every other moment is stored as the number of milliseconds since the epoch. A millisecond is a thousandth of a second, so one second is 1000, one day is 86,400,000, and the moment the exam starts — 9:30 am in India on Friday 20 November 2026 — is 1,795,147,200,000.

A moment written as that one number is called a timestamp. Date.now() gives you the timestamp of this very instant:

console.log(Date.now());   // a 13-digit number that grows by 1000 every second

Why store a moment as a plain count? Because counts are easy to work with. A later moment is simply a bigger number, so comparing two dates means comparing two numbers, and the time between them is one subtraction. Moments before 1970 are negative numbers, and nothing else changes.

Inside, every date is just one number: how many thousandths of a second have passed since the start of 1970. Everything else — the year, the month, "9:30 am", "Friday" — is worked out from that number when you ask for it.

Picture this. On a November afternoon, the first ball of a cricket final is bowled. A fan in Mumbai glances at her watch: 2:00 pm. A fan in Dubai sees 12:30 pm, and one in London sees 8:30 am. Nobody disagrees about when the ball was bowled; each of them is reading the same moment off a different clock. A Date is that moment. Ask it for the hour and it reads the moment off your computer's clock — section 62.8 shows how to read it off someone else's.
Quick check: which is the later moment, 1795147200000 or 1700000000000?

1795147200000. A timestamp only grows as time passes, so the bigger number is always the later moment. (The first is the exam; the second was a day in November 2023.)

62.2 Making a date: new Date, four ways

Date is a constructor built into JavaScript, so you make one with new, just as you made books with new Book(...) in Chapter 51. What you put in the brackets decides which moment you get:

You writeYou get
new Date()This moment, read from the computer's clock
new Date(0)The moment that many milliseconds after the epoch. 0 is the epoch itself; new Date(position.timestamp) turns a location reading into a date (Chapter 66)
new Date("2026-11-20T09:30")The moment written as text
new Date(2026, 10, 20, 9, 30)The moment built from parts: year, month, day, hours, minutes (seconds and milliseconds may follow)

The text must follow the ISO 8601 pattern: YYYY-MM-DD, biggest unit first, then if you like a T and the time as HH:MM. It is the one text format every browser reads in the same way. Other ways of writing a date are guesswork, and section 62.9 shows what Chrome makes of "20/11/2026".

The month that starts at zero

Now look hard at the parts form. The exam is in November, the eleventh month, yet the code says 10. In the parts form, months are counted from 0: January is 0, February is 1 and December is 11. The day, the hours and the minutes are counted the ordinary way. This is the most famous trap in JavaScript dates, and it has a reason: the month number was designed to be used as a position in an array of month names, and array positions start at 0 (62.3 shows it).

Wrong on purpose. new Date(2026, 11, 20) is 20 December, not 20 November. And new Date(2026, 12, 1) is not an error: month 12 is "one past December", so you get 1 January 2027. In the text form, by contrast, months are written normally: "2026-11-20" is November.

When you build a date from numbers, take one off the month: January is 0, December is 11. When you write a date as text, write the month as people do. Everything else is normal in both.

If the browser cannot understand what you gave it, you still get a Date object, but an empty one. It prints as Invalid Date, and its number is NaN (Chapter 46). Before trusting a date that came from a user, check it with Number.isNaN(d.getTime()).

62.3 Reading the parts: the getters

Once you have a date, a family of methods called getters reads its parts. These are the methods from your slide, applied to the exam date:

MethodGives youFor the exam (Fri 20 Nov 2026, 9:30 am)
getFullYear()The year, four digits2026
getMonth()The month, 0 to 11 (0 = January)10
getDate()The day of the month, 1 to 3120
getDay()The weekday, 0 to 6 (0 = Sunday)5
getHours()The hour, 0 to 239
getMinutes()The minutes, 0 to 5930
getSeconds()The seconds, 0 to 590
getTime()The timestamp in milliseconds1795147200000

The getters read the moment in the computer's own local time: Indian time on an Indian laptop. Two of them trip people up. getDate() is the date, the number on the calendar page. getDay() is the day of the week, counted from Sunday as 0, so Friday is 5. And avoid the old getYear(): it gives years since 1900, so it says 126 for 2026. Always write getFullYear().

Why count months and weekdays from 0? So that they can pick a name straight out of an array:

const months = ["January", "February", "March", "April", "May", "June", "July",
                "August", "September", "October", "November", "December"];
const days = ["Sunday", "Monday", "Tuesday", "Wednesday", "Thursday", "Friday", "Saturday"];

months[exam.getMonth()]   // months[10] is "November"
days[exam.getDay()]       // days[5] is "Friday"

When you need the month as a number for people, add one: exam.getMonth() + 1 is 11. (Chapter 47 turned day numbers into names with switch; the array does the same job in one line.)

62.4 Changing a date: setters, and adding days

Every getter has a matching setter: setFullYear, setMonth, setDate, setHours, setMinutes and setSeconds. A setter changes the Date object itself — it mutates it — and returns the new timestamp:

const d = new Date(2026, 10, 20, 9, 30);
d.setHours(14);                  // same day, now 2:30 pm
d.setMonth(11);                  // same date and time, now in December
console.log(d.toDateString());   // Sun Dec 20 2026

toDateString() gives just the date part in short English, which is handy in the Console.

The setters have one very useful habit: they roll over. Give a value past the end of its range and the date carries on into the next month or year, exactly as a calendar would. Day 32 of January is 1 February, and day 0 of a month is the last day of the month before. The parts form of new Date rolls over in the same way. That makes adding days a one-liner:

const due = new Date(2026, 10, 25);   // 25 November
due.setDate(due.getDate() + 14);       // "day 39 of November"...
console.log(due.toDateString());       // Wed Dec 09 2026 - rolled into December

November has 30 days, so day 39 is 9 December. You did not need to know that; the Date did. It works across the end of a year too, and for going back: d.setDate(d.getDate() - 7) is a week earlier.

To move a date by some days, take its day number, add (or subtract), and put the answer back with setDate. You never have to know how long a month is: if the number runs past the end, JavaScript counts on into the next month for you.

You may also see days added as milliseconds: new Date(d.getTime() + 14 * 86400000). That works in India, but in countries that change their clocks for summer, one day a year has 23 hours and another 25, so setDate is the safer habit.

Copy before you change

Because setters change the object, Chapter 51's warning about objects applies in full. = does not copy a Date; it gives the same Date a second name. To make a real copy, pass the old date to new Date:

const issued = new Date(2026, 10, 25);

const wrong = issued;              // a second name for the SAME Date
const right = new Date(issued);    // a NEW Date at the same moment

Change wrong and issued changes with it; change right and issued is safe. Notice too that const does not protect a date from its setters. const fixes which object the name points at, not what is inside the object.

Quick check: const d = new Date(2026, 11, 28); d.setDate(d.getDate() + 7); — what date is d now?

4 January 2027. Month 11 is December, which has 31 days, so "day 35 of December" rolls over into 4 January of the next year.

62.5 How long between? Differences, countdowns and age

Because a Date is a number underneath, you can subtract one date from another. The minus sign turns each date into its timestamp, so the answer is a number of milliseconds. Divide by the milliseconds in a day to get days:

const MS_PER_DAY = 24 * 60 * 60 * 1000;    // 86,400,000

const exam = new Date(2026, 10, 20);
const start = new Date(2026, 9, 30);        // 30 October
console.log(exam - start);                  // 1814400000 (on a computer in India)
console.log((exam - start) / MS_PER_DAY);   // 21

Hours, minutes and seconds work the same way: divide by 60 * 60 * 1000, 60 * 1000 or 1000. The comparisons <, >, <= and >= also turn dates into numbers, so exam > start is true. Two operators do not. === compares objects, not moments (Chapter 51), so two separate dates for the same instant are not ===; compare a.getTime() === b.getTime() instead. And + prefers joining text, so exam + 1 is the long date with a "1" stuck on the end, not one millisecond later.

Counting calendar days

A countdown such as "21 days to the exam" counts calendar days, and the time of day gets in the way. At 10:15 pm on 30 October, the 9:30 am exam on 20 November is only 20.47 days away by the clock, though everyone would call it 21 days, and rounding down would say 20. The fix is to move both dates to midnight before subtracting. Then round, to absorb the odd 23- or 25-hour day in countries that change their clocks:

function daysBetween(from, to) {
  const a = new Date(from.getFullYear(), from.getMonth(), from.getDate());  // midnight
  const b = new Date(to.getFullYear(), to.getMonth(), to.getDate());        // midnight
  return Math.round((b - a) / MS_PER_DAY);
}

Take one date away from another and you get milliseconds. Divide by 86,400,000 and you get days. If you want whole calendar days, set both dates to midnight first, so the time of day cannot spoil the count.

Age

Age is not "days divided by 365": leap years make that wrong on the days around a birthday. Work it out the way people do. Subtract the birth year from this year, then take one off if this year's birthday has not come yet:

function ageOn(birth, day) {
  let age = day.getFullYear() - birth.getFullYear();
  const hadBirthday = day.getMonth() > birth.getMonth() ||
    (day.getMonth() === birth.getMonth() && day.getDate() >= birth.getDate());
  if (!hadBirthday) age = age - 1;
  return age;
}

One more everyday use: to time how long some code takes, read Date.now() before and after, and subtract. Chapter 54 did exactly that to time a busy loop.

62.6 Showing dates to people

The default text, Fri Nov 20 2026 09:30:00 GMT+0530 (India Standard Time), is correct but unfriendly, and building a sentence from getters, as in 62.3, is slow work. The browser already knows how dates are written all over the world, in hundreds of languages. You only say whose way you want, with a locale code such as "en-IN", English as written in India. The two methods on your slide do the work:

CodeResult for the exam
exam.toLocaleDateString("en-IN")20/11/2026
exam.toLocaleDateString("en-US")11/20/2026 — Americans write the month first
exam.toLocaleTimeString("en-IN")9:30:00 am
exam.toLocaleTimeString("en-US")9:30:00 AM
exam.toLocaleString("en-IN")20/11/2026, 9:30:00 am — date and time together
Always name the locale. With empty brackets, toLocaleDateString() follows whatever the reader's computer is set to, so the same line prints 20/11/2026 on one laptop and 11/20/2026 on the next. For an Indian site, write "en-IN" and every reader sees the same thing.

A second argument, an options object, chooses which parts appear and how long each one is:

// Friday, 20 November 2026

exam.toLocaleTimeString("en-IN", { hour: "numeric", minute: "2-digit" })

Intl.DateTimeFormat: one formatter, many dates

Behind both methods stands Intl.DateTimeFormat, part of the browser's Intl toolbox. You can use it directly: build a formatter once, with a locale and options, then call its format method on as many dates as you like. That is tidier, and faster, when you format a whole timetable:

const fmt = new Intl.DateTimeFormat("en-IN", { dateStyle: "medium" });
fmt.format(exam);   // 20 Nov 2026

dateStyle and timeStyle are ready-made choices, each "full", "long", "medium" or "short".

62.7 Build it: a live clock with a greeting

Your second slide puts the chapter to work: show the current date and time, and keep the time display up to date every second. setInterval (Chapter 54) calls a function again and again at a fixed interval in milliseconds; here, 1000 milliseconds is one second.

One idea makes or breaks the clock. A Date does not tick. It is a snapshot of the moment it was made, like a photograph of a clock. So the clock must take a fresh reading, new Date(), inside the function that runs each second:

// WRONG: one snapshot, shown again and again - the clock is frozen
const now = new Date();
setInterval(() => { clock.textContent = now.toLocaleTimeString("en-IN"); }, 1000);

// RIGHT: a new reading every second
setInterval(() => { clock.textContent = new Date().toLocaleTimeString("en-IN"); }, 1000);

The greeting is a decision (Chapter 47) on getHours(), which runs from 0 to 23: before 12 is morning, before 17 (5 pm) is afternoon, and the rest is evening.

    timeEl.textContent = now.toLocaleTimeString("en-IN");
    dateEl.textContent = now.toLocaleDateString("en-IN",
      { weekday: "long", day: "numeric", month: "long", year: "numeric" });
  }

  tick();                                        // show it at once...
  const clockId = setInterval(tick, 1000);       // ...then every 1000 ms
  console.log("Clock started, timer ID:", clockId);

  document.getElementById("stop").addEventListener("click", () => {
    clearInterval(clockId);
    console.log("Clock stopped at", new Date().toLocaleTimeString("en-IN"));
  });
</script>

The Console first tests the decision: 9:00 gives Good morning, 13:00 gives Good afternoon, 20:00 gives Good evening. Then Clock started, timer ID: with a whole number. The page shows the right greeting for now, the time ticking once a second in the Indian style (4:07:52 pm, say) and today's date written out in full. Press Stop the clock and the Console records the moment it froze.

Three details are worth copying. The first tick() call fills the display at once. setInterval's delay is "at least 1000 ms", not exactly 1000 (Chapter 54), so a tick may come late; because each tick takes a fresh reading, the time shown is still right, and at worst a second is skipped. And the clock has no aria-live: a screen reader would otherwise announce it every second (Chapter 59).

Quick check: why does the script call tick() once before calling setInterval?

Because setInterval waits a full 1000 ms before its first call. Without the early tick(), the clock would sit empty for the first second.

62.8 Time zones: why India shows +05:30

Back to the cricket fans. The number inside a Date is the same everywhere on Earth: at 9:30 am in India it is 1,795,147,200,000 in Mumbai, in Tokyo and in New York. What differs is the wall clock. A time zone is a rule for turning that one number into a local time, written as an offset from UTC.

India's offset is +05:30: Indian Standard Time is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of UTC. The half hour comes from geography. The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, which is 15° every hour, and time zones follow longitude. India sets its clocks by the line of 82.5° East, which runs near Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh, and 82.5 ÷ 15 = 5.5 hours. The whole country shares this one zone and never changes its clocks for summer, so the offset is +05:30 all year. That is the GMT+0530 in every long date you have printed, and the reason the epoch showed 5:30 am.

JavaScript gives you both views of the moment:

CodeOn a computer in IndiaMeaning
exam.getHours()9The local hour. All the plain getters are local
exam.getUTCHours()4The hour at Greenwich. Each getter and setter for a part has a UTC twin, such as getUTCDate()
exam.getTimezoneOffset()-330Minutes to add to local time to reach UTC. India is 330 minutes ahead, so the sign is minus
exam.toISOString()2026-11-20T04:00:00.000ZAlways UTC. The Z means "zero offset"
exam.toLocaleString("en-IN", { timeZone: "Asia/Tokyo" })20/11/2026, 1:00:00 pmThe moment on another place's clock

toISOString() matters for one more reason. When a date is turned into JSON, to be saved in localStorage or sent to a server, JSON.stringify writes it as this ISO text. When it comes back through JSON.parse, it is still just text. Chapter 64 promised the cure, and it is short: pass the text to new Date() and you have a real date again.

The date-only trap. A date-only ISO string, new Date("2026-11-20"), is read as midnight UTC: 5:30 am on 20 November in India, and 7 pm on 19 November in New York — the wrong day. Add a time, "2026-11-20T00:00", and the same text is read as midnight local time. Watch for this with <input type="date">, whose value is exactly that date-only text: write new Date(input.value + "T00:00").

A Date holds one moment. The time zone only decides how that moment is shown. India shows it 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of the world's reference clock, which is why Indian dates carry +05:30.

<style> body { font-family: system-ui, sans-serif; padding: 14px; } </style>
<ul id="list"></ul>

<script>
  // This text names its own zone (+05:30), so it is the same moment on every computer
  const exam = new Date("2026-11-20T09:30:00+05:30");
  const list = document.getElementById("list");

  ["Asia/Kolkata", "UTC", "Asia/Tokyo", "America/New_York"].forEach((zone) => {
    const text = exam.toLocaleString("en-IN",
      { timeZone: zone, weekday: "short", hour: "numeric", minute: "2-digit" });
    console.log(zone + ":", text);
    const li = document.createElement("li");
    li.textContent = `${zone}: ${text}`;
    list.append(li);
  });

  console.log("The number:", exam.getTime());
  console.log("getHours():", exam.getHours(), "| getUTCHours():", exam.getUTCHours());
  console.log("getTimezoneOffset():", exam.getTimezoneOffset());
  console.log("toISOString():", exam.toISOString());

  // Into JSON (as localStorage would store it) and back again
  const saved = JSON.stringify({ paper: "CSE326", when: exam });
  console.log("Saved text:", saved);
  const back = JSON.parse(saved);
  console.log("After JSON.parse, when is a", typeof back.when);
  const restored = new Date(back.when);
  console.log("Restored:", restored.toLocaleString("en-IN"));

  // The date-only trap
  console.log("Date only:", new Date("2026-11-20").toString());
  console.log("With T00:00:", new Date("2026-11-20T00:00").toString());
</script>

The list and the Console agree: Asia/Kolkata: Fri, 9:30 am, UTC: Fri, 4:00 am, Asia/Tokyo: Fri, 1:00 pm and America/New_York: Thu, 11:00 pm — on any computer, because the text named its own offset. The number is 1795147200000, as always. On a computer in India the next lines are getHours(): 9 | getUTCHours(): 4, getTimezoneOffset(): -330 and toISOString(): 2026-11-20T04:00:00.000Z. The JSON text holds "when":"2026-11-20T04:00:00.000Z"; after parsing, when is a string; new Date turns it back into 20/11/2026, 9:30:00 am. Last, the trap in action: the date-only text gives 05:30:00, while the text with T00:00 gives 00:00:00.

62.9 Common misunderstandings

✗ "new Date(2026, 11, 20) is 20 November."
In the parts form, months count from 0, so 11 is December.
✓ "Months are 0 to 11 in parts and in getMonth(); people's months are getMonth() + 1."
Write 10 for November. In ISO text, "2026-11-20", months are written normally.
✗ "getDay() gives the day of the month."
For 20 November 2026 it gives 5.
✓ "getDate() is the date (1–31); getDay() is the weekday (0 = Sunday)."
And it is getFullYear(), never the old getYear().
✗ "Any sensible date text works: new Date("20/11/2026")."
Chrome reads it month-first, finds no month 20 and gives Invalid Date. Other non-ISO formats vary from browser to browser.
✓ "Use ISO text with a time, "2026-11-20T09:30", or the parts form."
A date-only ISO string means midnight UTC, which in India is 5:30 am.
✗ "const due = issued; gives me a copy I can change."
It is the same Date, so setDate changes both names.
✓ "Copy with new Date(issued), then change the copy."
Setters mutate the object, even one held in a const.
✗ "Two dates for the same moment are ===."
=== compares objects, and two Date objects are never the same object.
✓ "Compare a.getTime() === b.getTime()."
< and > do work on dates directly; subtracting gives milliseconds.
✗ "Make the Date once, and setInterval will keep it current."
A Date is a snapshot; it never moves by itself.
✓ "Call new Date() inside the function that runs every second."
And call that function once at the start, so the clock is never blank.

Exam-style checks

Answer each one before opening it. Most have four options and exactly one correct answer; three are short answers with no options.

For a date in March, what does getMonth() return?L1
  • A. 3
  • B. "March"
  • C. 2
  • D. "03"

Answer: C. Months count from 0: January 0, February 1, March 2. A is what people expect, which is exactly why it is the trap; the getter returns a number, not a name or text.

Which moment does new Date(2026, 0, 26) represent?L2
  • A. 26 January 2026, midnight
  • B. 26 February 2026, midnight
  • C. 26 December 2025, midnight
  • D. Invalid Date, because there is no month 0

Answer: A. Month 0 is January, so this is Republic Day 2026. Month 0 is perfectly valid; it is the first month, not a missing one.

Which method gives the day of the week, with 0 for Sunday?L1
  • A. getDate()
  • B. getWeekday()
  • C. getHours()
  • D. getDay()

Answer: D. getDay() is the weekday, 0 to 6. getDate() is the day of the month, 1 to 31, and getWeekday() does not exist.

What does Date.now() return?L2
  • A. A Date object for this moment
  • B. The number of milliseconds since 1 January 1970 UTC
  • C. The time now as text, such as "9:30 am"
  • D. The number of seconds since 1 January 1970 UTC

Answer: B. It is a plain number, the timestamp of now. new Date() is the one that makes a Date object (A), and the count is in milliseconds, not seconds (D).

What does this print? const d = new Date(2026, 10, 25); d.setDate(d.getDate() + 10); console.log(d.getDate(), d.getMonth());L3

Answer: 5 11. The date starts as 25 November (month 10). Day 35 of November rolls over, because November has 30 days, to 5 December. getDate() is 5 and getMonth() for December is 11.

What does this print? const a = new Date(2026, 10, 20); const b = new Date(2026, 10, 20); console.log(a === b, a.getTime() === b.getTime(), a <= b);L3
  • A. true true true
  • B. false true true
  • C. false false true
  • D. true true false

Answer: B. a and b are two separate objects, so === is false even though they hold the same moment. Their timestamps are equal, and <= compares the timestamps, so both of those are true.

Which line shows the exam date as 20/11/2026 on every reader's computer?L2
  • A. exam.toLocaleDateString()
  • B. exam.toString()
  • C. exam.toLocaleDateString("en-IN")
  • D. exam.toISOString()

Answer: C. Naming the locale fixes the format. A depends on each computer's settings and may print 11/20/2026; B gives the long programmer's format; D gives 2026-11-20T04:00:00.000Z.

A clock shows one time and never changes: const now = new Date(); setInterval(() => { clock.textContent = now.toLocaleTimeString("en-IN"); }, 1000); Why?L3
  • A. The Date was made once, outside the function, and a Date never updates itself
  • B. The interval should be 1, not 1000
  • C. toLocaleTimeString can only be called once
  • D. textContent cannot be changed by a timer

Answer: A. The function does run every second, but it shows the same snapshot each time. Move new Date() inside the function. B would call it every millisecond and still show the same snapshot.

On a computer in India, what does new Date().getTimezoneOffset() return?L2
  • A. 330
  • B. -330
  • C. 5.5
  • D. "+05:30"

Answer: B. The offset is in minutes, and it is the amount to add to local time to reach UTC. India is 330 minutes ahead of UTC, so the answer is minus 330. A has the sign the wrong way round.

On a computer in India, what does new Date("2026-11-20").getHours() return?L3
  • A. 0
  • B. 9
  • C. 5
  • D. NaN

Answer: C. A date-only ISO string means midnight UTC, which is 5:30 am in India, and getHours() reads local time. A is the trap: you would get 0 from "2026-11-20T00:00", which is read as local midnight.

What does this print? const issued = new Date(2026, 10, 25); const due = issued; due.setDate(due.getDate() + 14); console.log(issued.getDate());L3

Answer: 9. due = issued does not copy the Date; both names lead to one object. Adding 14 days to 25 November gives 9 December, and issued sees the change. const due = new Date(issued); would have kept the issue date at 25.

What does this print? console.log(new Date(2026, 2, 0).getDate());L3

Answer: 28. Month 2 is March, and day 0 of March rolls back to the last day of February. 2026 is not a leap year, so February ends on the 28th. That makes new Date(year, month + 1, 0).getDate() a quick way to count the days in any month.

Quick revision

Key takeaways

  1. A Date is one moment, stored as the milliseconds since 1 January 1970 UTC (the epoch). Date.now() gives that number for now; getTime() gives it for any date.
  2. new Date() is now; new Date(ms) is a timestamp; new Date("2026-11-20T09:30") is ISO text; new Date(2026, 10, 20, 9, 30) is parts — and months in parts count from 0.
  3. The getters read local time: getFullYear, getMonth (0–11), getDate (1–31), getDay (0–6, Sunday 0), getHours, getMinutes. The zero-based numbers index arrays of names; add 1 for a month number.
  4. Setters change the date itself and roll over, so d.setDate(d.getDate() + 14) adds 14 days across month and year ends. Copy first with new Date(old); = only shares.
  5. Subtracting dates gives milliseconds; divide by 86,400,000 for days, moving both to midnight for calendar days. < and > work; for equality compare getTime().
  6. Display with toLocaleDateString and toLocaleTimeString, always naming the locale ("en-IN"), plus an options object; reuse one Intl.DateTimeFormat for many dates.
  7. A live clock makes a fresh new Date() inside its setInterval callback and calls it once at the start.
  8. IST is UTC+05:30 (82.5° East ÷ 15° per hour). toISOString() and JSON are UTC; a date-only ISO string means UTC midnight.
