Accessibility Standards

Chapter 15 taught the habits; this chapter explains the rulebook behind them. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the international standard that laws, universities and companies point to when they say a site must be accessible. You will learn how WCAG is organised, what its four principles and three levels mean, the numbers you must remember (the contrast ratios), the companion standard ARIA, the laws that apply in India and abroad, and how to test a page.

20.1 WCAG: the standard

WCAG stands for Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. It is published by the W3C through its Web Accessibility Initiative (WAI), the same organisation that publishes the HTML and CSS standards (Chapter 1). Version 2.0 appeared in 2008, 2.1 in 2018 and 2.2 in 2023; each version adds criteria and keeps the earlier ones, so a page that meets 2.2 also meets 2.0. It is not "World Code Access Group", not "Web Compliance and Grading", and not an auto-generator.

20.2 The four principles: POUR

Everything in WCAG hangs off four principles, remembered as POUR. Exam questions describe a situation and ask which principle it tests, so learn the distinguishing idea of each.

Perceivable
Users must be able to perceive the content with at least one sense. Text alternatives for images, captions and transcripts for media, content that can be presented in different ways (read aloud, enlarged), and enough contrast between text and background.
Operable
Users must be able to operate the interface. Everything works from the keyboard alone; users get enough time; nothing flashes more than three times a second; pages are navigable with skip links, descriptive titles, a sensible focus order and visible focus; targets are big enough to hit. A user completing a checkout with only Tab and Enter is testing Operable.
Understandable
Users must be able to understand the content and the controls. The page's language is declared, navigation is consistent and predictable, form fields have labels and instructions, and errors are identified and explained.
Robust
Content must be robust enough to be interpreted reliably by a wide range of user agents, including assistive technologies: it keeps working across browsers, devices and screen readers, now and in future. In practice: valid HTML, native elements, correct name/role/value for every control.

20.3 Conformance levels: A, AA, AAA

Every success criterion is assigned a level.

LevelMeaningExample criterion
AThe minimum. Without these, some users cannot use the page at allImages have text alternatives; everything works from the keyboard
AAThe target most organisations and laws require. Removes the major barriers for most usersText contrast at least 4.5:1; visible focus; consistent navigation; captions for recorded video
AAAThe highest level; not required for whole sites because some content cannot meet itContrast 7:1; sign-language interpretation; reading level of lower secondary education

Conformance is cumulative: AA means all A and all AA criteria pass. There is no "Level B". When a policy, a tender or a law says "WCAG 2.x compliant", it almost always means Level AA.

20.4 The numbers: colour contrast

The most frequently checked WCAG criterion is contrast, because it has numbers. The contrast ratio between text and its background runs from 1:1 (identical) to 21:1 (black on white).

Normal text (below 18pt, or below 14pt bold)4.5 : 17 : 1
Large text (at least 18pt, or 14pt bold; roughly 24px / 18.5px)3 : 14.5 : 1
Icons, form borders, focus indicators (non-text contrast, WCAG 2.1)3 : 1

Contrast checkers (in Chrome DevTools, WebAIM's contrast checker, or the Lighthouse audit) compute the ratio for you. Two related rules: colour must never be the only way information is conveyed (add an icon or text to a red error), and users must be able to zoom text to 200% without losing content.

20.5 WAI-ARIA and the other companion standards

WAI-ARIA (Accessible Rich Internet Applications) is the W3C standard behind the role and aria-* attributes of Chapter 15. It defines roles (what an element is), states (checked, expanded) and properties (labelled-by, described-by) so custom widgets can be reported correctly. Its own guidance repeats the first rule of ARIA: use native HTML first, and never add a role an element already has (role="button" on a <button> is a mistake). Remember that ARIA changes only what assistive technology reports; keyboard behaviour still has to be provided.

Two siblings you may hear of: ATAG (guidelines for authoring tools such as CMS editors) and UAAG (guidelines for browsers and media players). For a web developer, WCAG plus ARIA is the working set.

20.6 Accessibility and the law

WCAG is a guideline, but laws around the world make it binding by reference.

WhereInstrumentWhat it requires
IndiaRights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016; the Guidelines for Indian Government Websites (GIGW); Indian Standard IS 17802 (based on WCAG)Government and public-facing ICT, including websites, must be accessible; GIGW requires WCAG conformance for government sites
United StatesAmericans with Disabilities Act (ADA); Section 508 of the Rehabilitation ActPublic accommodations and federal ICT must be accessible; Section 508 references WCAG 2.0 AA
European UnionEN 301 549; the Web Accessibility Directive; the European Accessibility Act (2025)Public-sector sites and many private services must meet WCAG 2.1 AA

The practical message: Level AA of the current WCAG version is the bar you build to, wherever your site will be used.

20.7 Testing a page

No tool can certify a page accessible, but a short routine catches most problems.

  1. Automated checks. Run Lighthouse (in Chrome DevTools), the WAVE browser extension or axe. They find missing alt text, missing labels, low contrast, missing lang, duplicate ids and invalid ARIA. Automated tools catch roughly a third of issues; the rest need a human.
  2. Keyboard only. Unplug the mouse. Tab through the page: can you reach every link and control, see where focus is, activate everything with Enter or Space, and escape from every menu and dialog?
  3. Screen reader. NVDA is free on Windows; VoiceOver is built into macOS and iOS; TalkBack into Android. Listen to the page: are landmarks, headings, images, links and form fields announced sensibly?
  4. Zoom and contrast. Zoom to 200%: nothing should overlap or vanish. Check contrast ratios of text and controls.
  5. Validate. Valid HTML is the Robust principle's foundation.

Exam-style checks

WCAG stands for:L1

  • B. Level AAA
  • C. Level AA
  • D. Level B

Answer: C. A is the minimum, AAA the ideal, and there is no Level B.

  • A. 7.0 : 1
  • B. 2.0 : 1
  • C. 3.0 : 1
  • D. 4.5 : 1

Answer: D. 3:1 is for large text; 7:1 is Level AAA.

A user completes a checkout using only the Tab and Enter keys. Which POUR principle is being tested?L2
  • A. Operable
  • B. Perceivable
  • C. Understandable
  • D. Robust

Answer: A. Keyboard operability is the heart of Operable.

Which POUR principle requires content to keep working across browsers, devices and assistive technologies?L2
  • A. Robust
  • B. Operable
  • C. Understandable
  • D. Perceivable

Answer: A.

Which organisation publishes open web standards such as HTML and the accessibility guidelines?L1

Answer: the W3C (World Wide Web Consortium), through its Web Accessibility Initiative for WCAG.

Web accessibility is the practice of building sites that:L2

Answer: people with visual, auditory, motor or cognitive disabilities can perceive, understand and use. Speed, ranking and browser version are not the definition.

Which statement about ARIA is correct, and which use of ARIA is a mistake?L3

Answer: ARIA changes how assistive technology reports an element but does not change its behaviour. Adding role="button" to a native <button> is a mistake; aria-describedby for a hint, aria-label on an icon button and aria-hidden on a decorative icon are correct.

A page's grey body text measures 3:1 against its background. Does it pass Level AA?L3

Answer: no. Normal text needs 4.5:1 at Level AA; 3:1 is enough only for large text (18pt, or 14pt bold) and for non-text elements such as icons and borders.

Quick revision

Key takeaways

  1. WCAG = Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, from the W3C/WAI; 2.2 (2023) is current. Structure: 4 principles, 13 guidelines, success criteria at levels A, AA, AAA.
  2. POUR: Perceivable (alt, captions, contrast), Operable (keyboard, skip links, focus), Understandable (lang, labels, errors), Robust (valid HTML, native elements, works across AT).
  3. Level AA is the target most organisations and laws require. Cumulative: AA includes A.
  4. Contrast: 4.5:1 normal text and 3:1 large text at AA; 7:1 / 4.5:1 at AAA; 3:1 for icons and borders.
  5. ARIA (WAI-ARIA) describes; native HTML first. Laws: RPwD Act 2016 and GIGW in India; ADA/Section 508; EN 301 549.
  6. Test: automated tool, keyboard only, screen reader, zoom and contrast, validate.
