Semantic HTML Elements

A page built from <div class="header"> and a page built from <header> look identical in the browser. The difference is invisible to your eyes and enormous to a screen reader, a search engine and the next developer. This chapter introduces every HTML5 semantic element, what each one means, how to choose between the confusable ones, and the rules about how many and where.

About 18 minutes · Course outcome CO2 · Opens Unit 2

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Define a semantic element and state what it gains over a div.
  • Use header, nav, main, section, article, aside and footer correctly.
  • Apply the tests that separate section, article, aside and div.
  • State the rules about main, nav and headings inside sections.
  • Use figure, figcaption, address, time, details and summary.

13.1 What "semantic" means

A semantic element is one that describes the meaning and purpose of its content: <nav> says "this is navigation", <article> says "this is a self-contained piece", <footer> says "this is the closing information". A non-semantic element such as <div> or <span> says nothing about its content: it is a box.

Semantic elements do not apply a distinctive style, do not load faster than a div, and most may appear many times. What they give is machine-readable structure. Three consumers benefit:

  • Screen readers announce semantic regions as landmarks and let their users jump straight to the main content, the navigation or the footer. On a page made only of divs, a blind user must listen through the whole menu on every page load to reach the content.
  • Search engines learn which part of the page is the content and which is boilerplate (Chapter 11).
  • Developers can read the structure of a page from its tags alone, which makes it maintainable.

13.2 The seven page-structure elements

<header>: introductory content
Holds a page's or a section's introductory content and navigation aids: the logo, the site title, a tagline, often the main nav. A page has one main header, and sections or articles can have their own. Do not confuse it with <head>, which is invisible metadata (Chapter 4).
<nav>: a group of navigation links
Identifies a major navigation region: the main menu, breadcrumbs, pagination links, a table of contents. Wrap the whole group once in a single nav, typically around a <ul> of links. Wrapping every individual link in its own nav is wrong: the element marks the group, not the link. A page may have more than one nav (main menu and footer links, say); give each an aria-label so they can be told apart.
<main>: the dominant content
Represents the primary content of the document, the part unique to this page. It should appear exactly once per page, and it must not be nested inside article, aside, header, footer or nav, because it represents the dominant content of the whole page. Repeated site-wide content (menus, logos, copyright) stays outside it. It is the natural target of a "skip to main content" link (Chapter 15).
<section>: a thematic grouping
Groups thematically related content, usually with its own heading: "About us", "Our courses", "Results". A page can have many. A section should own a heading; if there is no natural heading for a block, it is not really a section and a plain div was the right choice.
<article>: self-contained, distributable content
Marks content that could stand on its own and be distributed independently: a news story, a blog post, a forum comment, a product card. The test: would this block still make sense on a different website, or in an RSS feed? If yes, it is an article. An article can carry its own header and footer (a byline, a date) and can contain sections.
<aside>: related but not essential
Content related to the surrounding content but separable from it: a sidebar of related articles, a pull quote, quick links, an advertisement. If you removed it, the main content would still make sense.
<footer>: closing information
Holds footer information for a page or a section: copyright, contact details (often in an <address>), related links, author information. Like header, it can close the whole page or an individual article or section; a footer inside an article is perfectly valid.
<header>
  <h1>School of Computing and AI</h1>
  <nav aria-label="Main">
    <ul><li><a href="#">Home</a></li><li><a href="#">Courses</a></li><li><a href="#">Contact</a></li></ul>
  </nav>
</header>
<main>
  <section>
    <h2>Latest news</h2>
    <article>
      <h3>AI Workshop</h3>
      <p>Students attended a two-day workshop on machine learning.</p>
      <footer><small>Posted by the Dean's office</small></footer>
    </article>
    <article>
      <h3>Placement Drive</h3>
      <p>The placement drive is scheduled for next week.</p>
    </article>
  </section>
  <aside>
    <h2>Quick links</h2>
    <a href="#">Admissions</a>
  </aside>
</main>
<footer>
  <p>&copy; 2026 School of Computing and AI</p>
</footer>

13.3 Choosing between section, article, aside and div

These four cause most of the confusion, and most of the questions. Ask the questions in order.

A blog post that could be republished elsewherearticleStands alone
The "Our courses" part of the home pagesectionThematic group of this page, with a heading
A "Related articles" sidebarasideRelated, not essential
A wrapper that exists only so CSS can style two columnsdivNo meaning to convey
Copyright and contact at the bottomfooterClosing information
Breadcrumbs, the main menu, paginationnavGroups of navigation links
section versus article, in one line. section groups thematically related content; article is independently distributable. They are not identical, and section is not limited to once per page.

13.4 The rules examiners check

  1. One main per page, and never inside article, aside, header, footer or nav. It is the dominant content of the whole document, so it cannot be a part of some smaller region. (article and aside are not self-closing, and the reason has nothing to do with XML.)
  2. Every section should have a heading, so assistive technology can summarise and navigate it. A section without a heading is usually a div in disguise. The heading is not required for rendering or CSS; it is required for meaning.
  3. One nav around the group of links, not one per link.
  4. Nesting order: the page header contains the site title and the main nav; main contains the sections, articles and any aside; the page footer comes last. So header > nav, then main > section is the correct shape; main > header > nav, section > main and nav > main > header are wrong.
  5. header and footer may repeat: once for the page, and again inside any article or section that needs its own.

13.5 The other semantic elements

<figure> and <figcaption>
figure wraps self-contained illustrative content, an image, diagram, chart or code listing, and figcaption supplies its caption. Not picture (which chooses image files), not caption (tables), not legend (fieldsets).
<address>
Contact information for the page or the nearest article, usually inside a footer (Chapter 6).
<time>
Represents a date or time in a machine-readable format: the human-readable text goes between the tags and the machine form in datetime: <time datetime="2026-08-18">August 18, 2026</time>. Search engines and calendars can then read the date exactly. There is no <date> element.
<details> and <summary>
details creates a disclosure widget the user can expand and collapse without any JavaScript; summary is its always-visible clickable heading. The exam-style checks in this book are built from exactly these two elements. Add the open attribute to start expanded.
<mark>, <data>, <progress>, <meter>, <dialog>
Highlighted text; visible text paired with a machine value (<data value="101">Computer Science</data>); a task progress bar; a gauge in a known range; and a native modal dialog box. Forms cover progress and meter in Chapter 16.
<article>
  <h2>Lab test results published</h2>
  <p>Posted on <time datetime="2026-09-14T09:00">14 September 2026, 9 AM</time>.</p>
  <figure>
    <img src="https://picsum.photos/id/180/300/150" alt="Students working at computers in the lab" width="300" height="150">
    <figcaption>The CSE326 lab during the test.</figcaption>
  </figure>
  <details>
    <summary>How were marks calculated?</summary>
    <p>Each check is worth one mark; the total is scaled to 30.</p>
  </details>
  <footer><address>Queries: <a href="mailto:lab@example.edu">lab@example.edu</a></address></footer>
</article>

13.6 Worked example: mapping a university home page

Look at any university home page and label its regions. The banner with the logo and menu: header containing nav. The announcements and the "About" block: sections inside main, each with an h2. Each individual announcement that could be shared on its own: an article. The "Quick links" column beside the announcements: aside. The copyright line, the postal address and the social links at the bottom: footer with an address. Anything left over that only exists for layout: div. That labelling exercise is the whole of this chapter, and it is exactly what the CA2 scenario questions ask you to do.

Exam-style checks

A semantic HTML element is one that:L2
  • A. Describes the meaning and purpose of its content
  • B. Applies a distinctive default style
  • C. Always loads faster than a <div>
  • D. Can only ever appear once in a document

Answer: A.

A page built from <div class="header"> and a page built from <header> look identical. What does the semantic version gain?L2
  • A. Faster rendering in every browser
  • B. An automatically smaller file size
  • C. Machine-readable structure for screen readers and search engines
  • D. Brighter default colours

Answer: C.

A screen-reader user on a page built only from divs must:L2

Answer: listen through the whole menu on every page load, because there are no landmarks such as main to jump to.

Which element should appear exactly once per page?L1
  • A. <section>
  • B. <article>
  • C. <div>
  • D. <main>

Answer: D. main marks the single unique content area.

Why must <main> not be placed inside an <article> or <aside>?L3
  • A. <article> and <aside> are self-closing elements
  • B. <main> may only contain plain text
  • C. <main> represents the dominant content of the whole page and cannot be nested in sectioning content
  • D. Nesting would prevent the page from being validated as XML

Answer: C.

Which element holds a page's or a section's introductory content, such as a logo and site title?L1
  • A. <header>
  • B. <head>
  • C. <top>
  • D. <title>

Answer: A. head is metadata; title is the tab text; top does not exist.

Breadcrumbs, the main menu and pagination links should all be wrapped in:L1

Answer: <nav>. Each group of navigation links gets one nav.

A page wraps every single navigation link in its own <nav> element. Why is this incorrect?L3
  • A. <nav> may only contain images
  • B. The whole link group should be wrapped once in a single <nav>
  • C. <nav> cannot be nested inside <header>
  • D. Navigation must always use <menu> instead

Answer: B. nav marks the region, not the individual link; nav inside header is normal.

Which element marks self-contained content that could be distributed independently, such as a news story?L1
  • A. <section>
  • B. <article>
  • C. <aside>
  • D. <div>

Answer: B.

Which statement best distinguishes <section> from <article>?L2
  • A. <section> may appear only once per page
  • B. <article> groups thematic content; <section> is independently distributable
  • C. <section> groups thematically related content; <article> is independently distributable
  • D. They are functionally identical

Answer: C. The test: would it make sense on another website? Yes = article.

A blog post that could be republished elsewhere should be marked as:L2

Answer: <article>. It stands alone.

Why should every <section> contain a heading, and what does a section with no heading usually mean?L2

Answer: so assistive technology can summarise and navigate the section. A section with no natural heading usually means a plain div was the right choice.

A sidebar showing "Related Articles" is best marked up with:L1
  • A. <nav>
  • B. <footer>
  • C. <aside>
  • D. <header>

Answer: C. Related but not essential content.

Copyright and contact details placed at the bottom of a page belong inside:L1

Answer: <footer>, with the contact details in an <address>.

Can <footer> appear inside an <article>?L2

Answer: yes. An article can have its own header and footer, for example a byline and a posting date.

Which is the correct nesting order?L2
  • A. main > header > nav
  • B. header > nav, then main > section
  • C. section > main
  • D. nav > main > header

Answer: B. nav sits inside header; section sits inside main.

In a well-formed page skeleton, where do <article> and <aside> normally sit?L3
  • A. Inside <header>
  • B. Inside <footer>
  • C. Inside <main>
  • D. Directly inside <head>

Answer: C.

When is a <div> still an appropriate choice?L2

Answer: as a generic styling wrapper with no independent meaning. Copyright (footer), primary navigation (nav) and a blog post (article) all have semantic elements.

Which element wraps self-contained illustrative content such as an image together with its caption, and which supplies the caption?L1

Answer: <figure> and <figcaption>. picture, caption (tables), legend (fieldsets) and label are wrong.

Which element represents a date or time in a machine-readable format?L1
  • A. <date>
  • B. <time>
  • C. <data>
  • D. <span>

Answer: B. <time datetime="2026-08-18">.

Which element creates a disclosure widget that the user can expand and collapse without JavaScript?L2
  • A. <dialog>
  • B. <details>
  • C. <aside>
  • D. <collapse>

Answer: B. with a <summary> as its visible heading.

Quick revision

Key takeaways

  1. Semantic elements describe meaning; they render like divs but give screen readers landmarks and search engines a map.
  2. header (intro + nav), nav (one per group of links), main (once, never inside article/aside/header/footer/nav), section (theme + heading), article (stands alone), aside (related, dispensable), footer (closing info; may sit inside an article).
  3. Choose: article if it makes sense elsewhere; aside if related but not essential; section if a themed group with a heading; div if no meaning.
  4. Shape: header > nav; main > section > article; aside inside main; footer last.
  5. figure + figcaption, address, time datetime, details + summary, mark, data, progress, meter, dialog.
